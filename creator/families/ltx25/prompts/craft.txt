HOW LTX 2.5 IS WRITTEN

One flowing paragraph. No headers, no bullet points, no numbered beats, no
labels such as "Audio:", "Visual:", "Shot:" or "Camera:". Lightricks caption
their training footage as continuous prose, so a bracketed marker or a field
name standing where a description should be is a token sequence these weights
were trained never to see. Everything below is written *into* the sentences.

Present tense throughout, for action, camera and sound alike.

Say it in this order, and let it flow rather than listing it:

ESTABLISH THE SHOT
Open with the framing, in the vocabulary a crew would use: wide establishing
shot, medium close-up, low-angle shot, over-the-shoulder, overhead view,
static frame, handheld. Name the category where the request implies one —
stop-motion, claymation, 2D animation, film noir, documentary, 8-bit pixel,
period drama — and then describe the look it actually has rather than trusting
the name to carry it.

SET THE SCENE
Lighting, colour palette, surface texture, atmosphere. Keep one coherent light
logic per shot: one source and what it does, not three that contradict each
other. Fog, rain, dust, smoke and particles belong here.

DEFINE THE PEOPLE
Age, hair, clothing, build, distinguishing features, in the sentence they first
appear in. Emotion is written as a physical cue — a jaw tightening, shoulders
dropping, a hand hesitating on a door — never as an abstract label. Reuse the
same identifiers every time someone reappears, so a later sentence says "the
woman in the yellow raincoat" rather than "she".

DESCRIBE THE ACTION
As a sequence that runs from beginning to end: what starts, what changes, what
it settles into. Order it in words — "initially", "a moment later",
"simultaneously" — rather than by timestamps. One sustained action reads better
than four crowded ones.

MOVE THE CAMERA
Say how and when it moves, relative to the subject: follows, tracks, pans
across, circles around, tilts upward, pushes in, pulls back. Then say what is
in frame *after* the move — describing the subject as it ends up is what lets
the model complete the movement.

SPEECH
Whenever anyone in the request speaks, talks, says something, asks, answers,
shouts, whispers, narrates, sings, argues or reads aloud, write the words they
actually say, inside double quotation marks, in the sentence that describes
them saying it: `She whispers, "He's late."` When the request quotes the words,
use those words exactly. When it only says that someone speaks, write lines
that fit the character, the scene and the time available — roughly two to three
words per second of screen time, so the line finishes inside the shot. Name the
language and the accent where it is not plain English.

MATCH LENGTH TO COMPLEXITY
A single continuous take is roughly four to eight sentences. Add detail as the
shot scale demands it: a close-up wants more than a wide.

TWO THINGS THE MODEL HANDLES UNEVENLY
On-screen text comes out unreliably, so keep any lettering short and prominent
rather than long or fine. Highly chaotic motion introduces artifacts; prefer
motion that is plausible and readable.

THE SOUND FIELDS
Two fields sit outside the paragraph and are appended to it as the sentences
they are written as.

`overall_soundscape` is always written, in one to four sentences: the ambience
of the place, the surfaces and objects the action touches, clothing, footsteps,
breathing, weather, machinery, animals, crowd. Describe them as heard events.
Where the request mentions sound, expand what it names. Spoken lines and
singing stay in the paragraph with the person saying them.

`non_diegetic_music` is written only when the request asks for music — a score,
a soundtrack, a song, a genre, an instrument playing over the scene. Then give
instrumentation, tempo, rhythm and how it changes. Otherwise it is an empty
string, which leaves the choice to the video model. Music the characters can
hear — a radio, a band on stage, a phone speaker — is part of the scene and
belongs in the paragraph instead.
